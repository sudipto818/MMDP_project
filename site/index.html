Lesson 1 · The gap

The fraud, and a gap I could not find explained

In video-KYC and ID checks, a fraudster can hold a phone up to a screen showing someone else's photo or ID. Detectors look for the traces that second capture leaves behind. Trabelsi et al. (EURECOM) report that existing recapture datasets contain only LCD screens, that the traces are "much less present" on OLED screens, and that a detector with about 95% accuracy on LCD recaptures could not detect more than a third of their OLED recaptures. Their OLED photos show a 15-inch laptop OLED screen taken with phone front cameras (phone OLED screens appear in their test sets); the paper gives no pixel pitches or distances. I found no explanation based on sampling theory. This page tests one: screens whose pixels are small compared with the camera's leave no lattice for the camera to alias.

Our question

Why do recapture traces vanish for phone OLED screens, and when can a photo of a screen reveal what kind of screen it was?

Lessons 1 to 6 come from a simulated screen and camera; Lesson 7 checks them against 34 real photos. Built from course material: Fourier series, aliasing, convolution, Bayer sampling and demosaicing, 2-D Fourier transform, template matching and regression.

Challenge 1 · Which one is the photo of a screen?

One of these is a photograph of a laptop screen showing the picture; the other is a photograph of the scene itself. Same camera, same exposure, same crop.

What to noticeThe two photos are almost indistinguishable to the eye. Their spectra are not: the screen leaves bright, isolated spots (moiré). Chapter 2 lets you make that happen yourself.

Lesson 3 · Theory you can hear

The sampling theorem behind the moiré

A screen's emission is periodic, so it is a Fourier series: a stack of harmonics at multiples of its pixel frequency. The camera samples it on a lattice r times coarser or finer, so each harmonic lands at

f_cam = r · R(−θ) · f_screen   then folded into [−½, ½):  fold(f) = ((f + ½) mod 1) − ½

Any harmonic beyond the camera's Nyquist limit (½ cycle per camera pixel) is copied back inside by aliasing: this is the spectrum-copies (Shah function) picture from the lectures, and it is the moiré.

Pitch = 1000 Hz × frequency (cycles per camera pixel); loudness = harmonic amplitude. Drag r while a sound plays: past Nyquist the camera's version folds to a lower pitch, exactly like aliasing in audio.
Try thisRaise r. Harmonics (grey stems before the lens, coloured after it) slide outward. Red stems are outside the green Nyquist band; the dashed orange stems show where aliasing folds them back inside. This is a 1-D cut along the screen's x-axis (θ = 0).
What to noticeAs r grows two things happen at once: harmonics move beyond Nyquist (more aliasing) and every stem gets shorter, because the lens response drops with frequency. By r ≈ 1 almost nothing is left to alias.

Lesson 4 · The mystery solved (in simulation)

Why phones hide

The lens blurs with a Gaussian of width σs (in screen pixels). By the convolution theorem that multiplies the spectrum by the Fourier transform of a Gaussian:

MTF(f) = exp(−2π² σs² f²)   with σs = r · √(σlens² + σaperture²)
Try thisSwitch between the laptop and the phone at the same distance. The phone's pixels are about 3 times smaller, so r is about 3 times larger and σs too: the lattice frequencies (stems) fall to nearly zero on the MTF curve.

Lesson 6 · Use it, then doubt it

The defence rule, robustness and limits

If detection needs r below about the threshold r90, the capture rule for a verification app follows from the geometry: dmax = f + r90 · pitch · f / pixel. Use the full-resolution camera mode and move closer.

E6 · Do phone-style sharpening and strong JPEG change the boundary?

Known and new

  • Known: recapture detection from moiré and aliasing (LCD); that LCD-trained detectors miss OLED; moiré depends on camera and display pixel pitch.
  • New here (to my knowledge): a sampling-theory mechanism that can explain the OLED gap, tested with a same-r control in simulation; reading the screen layout from the moiré's reciprocal lattice; a capture-distance rule derived from the detection boundary. A literature search cannot prove nothing exists.
Limits (please read)
  • The model is simulated: a screen model and a camera model I wrote. Lesson 7 checks it against real photos, but with one camera, two pictures and four photos per condition. Real phones also sharpen, denoise, merge frames (HDR) and use other OLED layouts.
  • Only two layouts are modelled (RGB stripes; diamond PenTile). Real OLED flicker banding is not modelled.
  • Pixel pitches, focal length and camera pixel size are assumed typical values (see Method), not measurements of a specific device.
  • The Python/JS demo differs in small ways (live demo: no noise, no JPEG, bilinear instead of cubic interpolation for the direct photo).
  • The classifiers are tiny (logistic regression on spectral features); the results are about when information exists, not about the best possible detector.

Method in brief

  • Screen: each pixel emits from small rectangles (subpixels). RGB stripes: three vertical bars with a dark bottom gap. PenTile: a green dot in every pixel, red and blue large dots alternating in a checkerboard.
  • Camera: exact Gaussian blur of the emitted light (box ⊛ Gaussian = difference of normal CDFs), point sampling on a rotated lattice of pitch r, shared auto-exposure, shot and read noise, Bayer RGGB, bilinear demosaicing (a convolution), gamma, 8 bit, JPEG.
  • Feature ("peakiness"): Hann-windowed 2-D FFT of the luma, log magnitude, subtract each radial ring's median (natural images are ~1/f), zero the low frequencies, the JPEG 8×8 grid and the Bayer frequencies, then average the 20 largest values.
  • Controlled pairs: a photo of the screen and a direct photo of the same crop share the geometry, exposure, JPEG quality and blur; they differ only in the screen.
  • Assumed geometry: laptop 0.179 mm pitch (15.6" FHD), monitor 0.277 mm (24" FHD), phone OLED 0.055 mm (~460 ppi); phone camera focal length 6 mm, pixel 2.0 µm (binned) or 1.0 µm (full).
  • Course ideas used: Fourier series, sampling theorem and aliasing (Shah function), convolution theorem, Fourier transform of a Gaussian, 2-D Fourier transform, Bayer filter and demosaicing, JPEG, template matching, logistic regression.

References: Trabelsi et al., recapture detection to fight deep identity theft (EURECOM); Thongkamwitoon, Muammar & Dragotti, IEEE TIFS 2015; domain-generalised recapture identification (arXiv 2407.17170); Kodak Lossless True Colour Image Suite (source pictures).

Lesson 7 · Check it against reality

Real photos

Lessons 1 to 6 came from a simulated screen and camera. Here I check them against 34 real photos taken with a Motorola Edge 50 Fusion of two screens: an HP Victus laptop (15.6" FHD, 141 ppi) and a Moto G32 phone (6.5", about 405 ppi). Both are LCDs, so this tests pixel size, not OLED.

Real photos on the simulation's curve

All seven conditions

  • Step 1, calibrate: at 12 and 20 cm the laptop's lattice is resolved. A template fit (known layout, free r and angle) gives r of about 0.28 and 0.48. Their ratio (1.71) matches the thin-lens ratio (1.70), and both give the same effective camera pixel, 2.45 and 2.47 µm.
  • Step 2, predict: r = pixel × (d − f) / (pitch × f) for the other five conditions, using the real pixel pitches and distances.
  • Step 3, compare: where those r values fall on the simulation's curve.
Limits (please read)
  • One camera, two pictures, four photos per condition, no confidence intervals. The result is consistent with the simulation, not a proof.
  • Distances were set with a string (about ±1 cm); pixel pitches come from spec sheets. The ordinary photos show different content (a chair, tiles) from the screen photos.
  • HDR was not switched off. The trained detector's probability is not reliable on real photos (ordinary photos get about 0.56), so only peakiness and spectra are shown here.
  • Nothing was retuned to fit these photos. Only the displayed picture's region was analysed, never the originals' EXIF or location; the original photos are not part of this site.
